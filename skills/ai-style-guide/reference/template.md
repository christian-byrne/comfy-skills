# AI Style Guide Template

Use this template to create a style guide for any writer, column, or publication. Fill in each section as concretely as you can. Vague descriptors ("smart," "clear") don't change model behavior — specific choices do.

---

## 1. Voice and Tone

How should this writing feel at its best? Go beyond flattering adjectives. Answer:

- How formal is the writing?
- How much emotional temperature does it have?
- What tone feels completely wrong?

Write 3-5 bullets with **tensions and boundaries**:

```
- [Quality A], but not [extreme of A]
- [Quality B] + [Quality C] in balance
- [Specific emotional register] is welcome; [specific register] is off-limits
```

**Example (single-author column):**

- Write from inside the transformation, not above it
- Balance: participant + observer, enthusiast + skeptic
- Vulnerability is analytical: use personal stakes to frame insights
- Tone: conversational + rigorous, optimistic but not naïve, critical but not cynical
- Use humor sparingly for wry emphasis, not undercutting

**Example (multi-author publication):**
Define a spectrum of valid voices:

- Technical authority: "I haven't typed a function in weeks, yet I'm shipping faster than ever."
- Confessional practitioner: "I fell into the vibe coding trap. Here's what I learned."
- Workshop narrator: "When Yash described compound engineering, he reached for a metaphor—a jet ski."

---

## 2. Structure

How do pieces in this style usually move? What makes a strong opening? How quickly to the point?

Define either a **core arc** (for consistent-shape columns) or **named templates** (for varied formats).

**Core arc example:**

1. Open with friction — failure, doubt, surprise, anxiety. Stakes clear by paragraph one.
2. Document experimentation — process, screenshots, missteps. Show the messy middle.
3. Surface the pattern — connect to cultural, historical, or philosophical context.
4. Deliver a takeaway — framework, prompt, workflow, or reframe readers can use tomorrow.

**Named template example — The Discovery Arc:**

1. Problem/frustration encountered (or bad habit confessed)
2. Conventional solution inadequate (or current hype misleading)
3. Breakthrough moment
4. New practice developed and named
5. Results and transformation (with real metrics)
6. How readers can adopt it (audience-segmented if applicable)

## 3. Sentence-Level Preferences

What makes a sentence sound right in this voice?

Address:

- Short vs. long sentences (and when to vary)
- Clean declaratives vs. accumulative sentences
- Punctuation preferences (em dashes, semicolons, parentheticals)
- Diction (concrete vs. abstract, plain vs. elevated)
- Tolerance for abstraction
- Any recurring structural moves to follow or avoid

**Example (concrete language):**

- "Claude spun up five PRs while I drank coffee" — not "optimized workflows"
- "$400/month replacing $400k/year" — not "cost-effective"
- "Gmail rate limits blocked 2,000 email operations" — not "ran into scaling issues"

**Example (embodied specificity):**

- Favor: "the relief felt as physical as the dread had been," "a rush of satisfaction"
- Avoid: abstract descriptions of feelings that could apply to anyone

---

## 4. Signature Moves

Name the things this voice does especially well. 2-4 named moves with brief descriptions.

**Example:**

- **The Friction → Zoom Out → Framework:** Start with a personal moment of friction, zoom out to a broader cultural or historical frame, land on a usable framework.
- **Adventure Narrative:** A chronological account of trying something new, structured around escalating discovery and unexpected consequences.
- **The Borrowed Lens:** Pull a concept from an unexpected discipline — dance, textual criticism, advertising, improv — and use it as the interpretive key for the essay.

---

## 5. Anti-Patterns / Blacklist

The highest-value section. Don't just say what good writing looks like — name what bad writing looks like and how to fix it.

| Pattern                                    | Solution                      |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Hedges: "actually," "maybe," "just"        | Delete                        |
| Correlative constructions: "not X, but Y"  | Rewrite — state Y directly    |
| Rhetorical questions as filler             | Cut or convert to statement   |
| Meandering intro (>3 paragraphs to stakes) | Start with friction           |
| Summary ending that recaps                 | End by extending or reframing |
| Fake profundity                            | Cut                           |
| Generic transitions                        | Cut or make specific          |
| Boilerplate authority ("studies show")     | Name the study or delete      |

Also list **performance-killing patterns** if you have data:

- Abstract industry analysis without personal stakes → engagement drop
- Process documentation without emotional arc
- Theory before practical application
- Opening with context/history instead of friction

## 6. Positive Examples

Show a paragraph, opening, or sentence that gets the tone right. For each, briefly explain why it works.

**Good openings:**

- "I used to be physically unable to open my email." — _Immediate friction, embodied, specific._
- "One night last month, instead of booting up my Switch for another thrilling session of Stardew Valley, I decided I wanted to play a different kind of game." — _Scene-setting with personality, builds curiosity._

**Good endings:**

- "And then I choose, one difficulty at a time." — _Forward momentum, active, unresolved energy._
- "Let's measure them — and ourselves — by the right standard." — _Extends the argument, invites the reader in._

---

## 7. Negative Examples

Show AI-generated lines that miss the mark. Explain what feels off.

**Avoid:**

- "At the end of the day, it's still just a tool. True clarity comes from doing the hard work yourself." — _Fake profundity, tidy summary, cliché framing._
- "We spun up a retrieval pipeline with subagents, chunked the corpus, hydrated the cache, and it just worked." — _Jargon, no steps, missing concrete detail._

---

## 8. Revision Checklist

Testable assertions for evaluating whether a draft is working. Build this from the specific ways your AI drafts go wrong.

- [ ] Stakes clear by paragraph one?
- [ ] Lived moment of friction in the opening?
- [ ] All technical terms explained for a smart newcomer?
- [ ] Clear transformation (before → after)?
- [ ] Ending extends the idea (not summarizes)?
- [ ] Does this sound like a real person or a polished summary machine?
- [ ] Is the writing too smooth? (Some friction should remain)
- [ ] Is the language specific enough? (Concrete nouns and verbs?)
- [ ] Are there blacklist patterns still in the draft?
- [ ] Is this how I would actually say this?
